CHAPTER THREE

The Chocolate Touch

The morning everything turned sweet

Small group · Grades 3–4 · 50 minutes

Have this up before they walk in. The three hook objects (pencil, water bottle, shoe) are already on the table, covered with a paper towel or just sitting there. Say nothing about them yet — let them wonder. Do not read the title slide aloud. Kids who see the word "chocolate" will start talking, and that is exactly the energy you want. Let them buzz for fifteen seconds, then move. Quick setup check before you start: menu paper folded and stacked, index cards out, the four vocabulary cards face down, the book at Chapter 3. Allergy reminder: if you are using real chocolate as the focus treat, confirm your list today, not in the moment.

TODAY'S BIG QUESTION

Is John lucky
or unlucky?

Keep this question in your head the whole time we read.

Say it out loud, then say it again. This question is the spine of the entire lesson and of the whole book. Tell them straight: "I am not going to tell you the answer today. You are going to decide, and at the end you are going to write your answer on a card. Then later this week we will find out who was right." That framing does two things. It gives every kid a reason to listen, and it makes the Chapter 4 reveal land like a plot twist instead of just more reading. Do not let anyone answer yet. If someone shouts "lucky!" say "Hold that. Write it in your brain. I want your reason, not just your guess." Timing: 1 minute. Move.

The Chocolate Touch Test

One of these is about to turn into chocolate. Which one is best? Which one is a disaster?

✏

A pencil

💧

A water bottle

👟

A shoe

1 · Hook · 5 minutes

Hold up the real objects, not just the slide. Physical objects beat pictures for this group every time. Run it fast. "Pencil. Chocolate. Good idea or bad idea?" Take a show of hands, then ask one kid why. Repeat for the bottle and the shoe. The argument you want to surface: the shoe sounds funny until you realize you cannot walk. The water bottle sounds great until you are thirsty. Do not say this yourself. Wait — someone will get there, and if they do not, ask "But what if you were really thirsty?" and let it land. This is the whole book in ninety seconds. You are planting the idea that a good thing can go wrong when there is too much of it, without ever using the word "theme." Do not resolve the argument. End with: "A boy named John Midas is about to find out. Let's go." If the group is chatty, cap it at three speakers and move on. Better to leave them wanting to argue than to burn eight minutes here.

Four words, four moves

transform

to change into something else

Move: hands morph in the air

indulge

to enjoy something way too much

Move: rub your belly

wasteful

using more than you need

Move: toss it over your shoulder

suspicious

thinking something is not right

Move: narrow your eyes

2 · Words · 5 minutes

Teach the move with the word, every time. The motion is not decoration — it gives kids who lose the word a way to hold onto the meaning, and it gives you a silent check for understanding later in the lesson. Run the round: you say a sentence, they throw the motion. No talking, just hands. "John squeezed out half the tube of toothpaste." (wasteful) "The toothbrush turned brown." (transform) "Mary looked at John like he was up to something." (suspicious) "John ate the whole tube." (indulge) "The eggs became chocolate eggs." (transform) Go fast, three or four rounds, and let it get a little silly. Silly is fine. You are building automaticity. One honest caveat: these are concept words chosen for the ideas in this chapter, not words pulled verbatim from the text. Glance at your copy this morning and swap in anything your students actually stumble on. If a student has a specific vocabulary goal on their IEP, front-load that word here instead of one of these. Callback later: when you get to the Reader's Theater, ask "Which word fits Mary right now?" They should throw the suspicious eyes.

3 · Read aloud · 12 minutes

You read, they follow. Do not round-robin this chapter — cold popcorn reading kills the pacing and humiliates the kids who are decoding slowly. They get their turn to read out loud in the Reader's Theater, which is rehearsed and safe. Read with real commitment. John is delighted. Mary is exasperated. Mrs. Midas is distracted and pleased. If you perform it, they will perform it back to you in twenty minutes. Hold up fingers for the stop number as you approach each one so the group can see it coming. The three stop slides are next — advance to each one when you get there, then come back to reading. Press O for the overview if you need to jump. If your group has a student who tracks poorly, give them a fingertip or an index card to slide down the page. If you have a student who needs the text in hand, make sure they have their own copy open, not a shared one. Watch the clock. Twelve minutes covers the read plus the three stops. If you are running long, cut stop 1 (it is the least loaded) and keep stops 2 and 3.

STOP 1 — THE TOOTHPASTE

Is this good news
for John?

👍

Good news

👎

Bad news

Hands only · then one reason out loud

Everyone votes with a thumb. Everyone. Scan the group and make sure no hand is missing — a kid who does not vote is usually a kid who is lost, and that is your cue to back up two sentences. Expect almost all thumbs up. That is fine and that is the point. This is the chapter where the chocolate touch looks like a pure win. Then take exactly one "why." Ask a kid who voted thumbs up: "What is good about it?" If anyone votes thumbs down, that is gold. Go to them second and ask why. Something like "toothpaste is not food" or "he is going to get a stomachache." Say back to the group: "Hold on. She thinks this might be a problem. Everybody remember that." Do not correct anyone. There is no right answer yet. Thirty to sixty seconds, then back to reading.

STOP 2 — THE ORANGE JUICE

“Boy, that's good.
Chocolate juice.”

What does John think is happening?

What do we think is happening?

The gap between those two answers is the whole book

This is the most important sixty seconds in the lesson. Slow down here. John thinks: this is amazing, this is lucky, breakfast is delicious. He has no idea anything is wrong. We think: wait, that is orange juice. Orange juice is not supposed to be chocolate. Something is happening to him. Name it for them in plain words: "We know something John does not know. That is a feeling authors do on purpose, and it is why this book is funny." You do not have to say "dramatic irony" — but if you have a student who loves a big word, give it to them, they will hoard it. Sentence frames if they stall: "John thinks ______, but I think ______." "John is happy because ______. I am worried because ______." If a kid says "he is being tricked" or "it is magic" — push once: "Who is doing it? Where did it come from?" They will not know. Good. That is Chapter 2's candy store coming back, and it is the question that carries them forward. Ninety seconds max, then keep reading.

STOP 3 — MRS. MIDAS

“I'm very pleased with you this morning.”

She is happy with him. Should she be?

Turn and talk · 45 seconds · then two shares

Turn and talk for this one. Partners, forty-five seconds, then two pairs share. The trap to walk them into: John finished his breakfast and cleaned his plate, so from his mom's side he looks like a model kid. But he only ate it because it turned to chocolate. He is not being good. He is being lucky. Ask: "Did John do the right thing, or did he just get what he wanted?" That distinction is hard and worth the time. Connect back to the word wall: which of our four words fits Mrs. Midas right now? Not suspicious — she should be, and she is not. That is the joke. Optional push for a strong group: "Whose job is it to notice something is wrong? Mary noticed. Why doesn't anyone listen to Mary?" Kids with older siblings will have opinions. Then finish the chapter. Do not stop again — let the ending run clean into the activity.

Which one makes you hungry?

VERSION A

Chocolate eggs.
They are good.

VERSION B

Chocolate eggs — so warm and gooey that they melt before your fork gets there.

Chocolate ______ — so ______ that it ______ in your mouth.

4 · Model · 3 minutes

Read Version A out loud in the most bored voice you own. Then ask: "Does that make you want to eat it?" They will say no. Ask why not, and let THEM tell you it does not say anything. Then read Version B like you mean it. The difference will be obvious without you explaining a rubric. The point to name, once, in plain language: "Good writing makes a picture in someone else's head. Version A does not make a picture. Version B does." Then build one together on the board using the frame, with the whole group feeding you words. Take their worst suggestion seriously and make it work — that is what tells the reluctant kid it is safe to try. Do not build more than one together. If you model three, they will copy your three. The frame stays visible during the activity. Leave this slide up, or write the frame on the board before you advance.

The Midas Breakfast Menu

Design the breakfast John ate. Three items. Make them sound impossible to resist.

1

Fold your paper like a menu

2

Write 3 breakfast foods, chocolate-ified

3

Use the frame for each one

Done early? Illustrate it, or add a Warning line at the bottom.

5 · Menu · 15 minutes

This is the longest work block. Circulate, do not sit. What you are looking for as you move: a kid with a blank page after two minutes. That is not defiance, it is idea-generation load. Hand them the pre-filled menu from the packet (chocolate toast, chocolate eggs, chocolate juice already written in) so they only have to write the description. That single swap is the difference between finishing and shutting down. For a student who needs scribing: let them say the whole menu to you, you write item one, they copy it, you write items two and three. They still did the thinking. Conferencing line that works better than praise: "Read me your favorite one." Then, "What does it taste like when it is in your mouth?" and write down the exact words they say. Hand it back: "Put that in. Those were your words." The Warning line is quiet foreshadowing. Kids who write "Warning: you might never eat real food again" are doing genuine prediction work. Point that out loud so the others hear it. At twelve minutes give a two-minute warning. At fifteen, stop them mid-sentence if you have to. Unfinished is fine — the menus go home or in the folder. If the energy is high and they are producing, you can steal three minutes from the Reader's Theater. Do not steal from the exit ticket.

Reader's Theater: the breakfast table

John

Delighted. Cannot believe his luck.

Mary

Annoyed. Telling on him. Big-sister energy.

Mrs. Midas

Busy. Pleased. Not really looking.

Perform it once. Swap roles. Perform it again.

6 · Perform · 8 minutes

The swap is not optional. Reading the same lines twice is the fluency work, and the role change is what keeps it from feeling like a drill. Nobody notices they are rereading. Coach exactly one thing: tone. Do not correct decoding errors during a performance, ever. If a word breaks, supply it instantly and keep going. Fluency practice dies the second it turns into a correction session. The coaching line that gets results: "Mary, you are TELLING ON HIM. Make me believe you are the big sister." Then let them redo just that line. Kids will ham it up, and the hamming is the comprehension — you cannot perform a tone you do not understand. Group of four or five? Add a Narrator role, or double-cast John so two kids read his lines in unison. Unison reading is a soft landing for your most hesitant reader and it does not single anyone out. Group of two? You take Mrs. Midas. Tie it back to the words: after the performance ask, "Which of our four words fits Mary?" You want the suspicious eyes thrown back at you. If you are short on time, cut the second round, not the first. But try not to — the second round is where it actually gets good.

Exit ticket — make your call

1. Is John LUCKY or UNLUCKY? Circle one.

2. One reason from the chapter.

Because ______________________________

7 · Exit ticket · 5 minutes · I am keeping these

Say the magic words: "I am keeping these. You will see this card again." That sentence is what makes the exit ticket feel like a bet instead of a worksheet. Kids write more carefully when they think they are going to be held to it. Circling is the whole first answer, so there is no writing barrier on the part that matters most. Everyone can do question one. Question two is where you differentiate — a single word is an acceptable reason for a student who is drained by now. Accept oral answers and scribe them. Write exactly what they say and read it back. Do not coach toward "unlucky." Most of them will say lucky, because in this chapter he IS lucky. A room full of "lucky" cards is a correctly-calibrated group, not a failure. Put the cards somewhere you will actually find them. Chapter 4 is where the glove turns to chocolate and Spider Wilson takes a bite, and handing these back right before you read it is the single best five seconds of the unit. The kids who wrote "lucky" will physically react. Note who wrote a reason tied to the text versus a reason tied to their own feelings about chocolate. That is your quick formative read on inferencing, and it costs you nothing extra.

NEXT TIME

John puts on his gloves.

Everything is still going great for him.
It is about to stop going great for him.

Leave them on the cliff. Do not explain it. "Tomorrow John puts his gloves on. That is all I am telling you." Then close the book. The kid who groans is the kid who is hooked. Your own to-do before the next session: Keep the exit tickets somewhere you will find them — they are the opener for Chapter 4. Menus go home, in folders, or on the wall. If you post them, post every single one. Note anyone who could not get started on the menu without the pre-filled version, and start them on the pre-filled version next time without making it a thing. If you want a bridge activity between sessions, the one-sentence prediction on the back of the exit card does it: "What is the first thing that will go wrong?"
